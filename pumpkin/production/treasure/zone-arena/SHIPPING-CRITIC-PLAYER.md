# Blind player critic

Evidence checked: SHIPPING-BLIND-PACK.md only. All judgments and scores below are INFERRED simulated player reactions. No teen interviews, playtest, runtime checks, source inspection, or measured retention.

Lens: a player arriving from a short TikTok clip, deciding quickly whether stealing and escaping feels worth repeating.

| Option | Overall /10 | Difficulty quality /10 | Shipping simplicity /10 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| X | 8 | 7 | 9 |
| Y | 7 | 7 | 7 |
| Z | 6 | 6 | 4 |

Winner: X. I can see where the treasure is, why the next stretch looks harder, and where to run home. That makes its first clip easiest to understand. Its straight sightline also gives the visible warning and locked-aim rules their best chance to feel fair. Simplicity scores compare these proposals; they do not validate builder-day estimates.

X hook/repeat: stealing, waking the Keeper, baiting its dash into a wall, then reaching the pink home lip is a readable little story. The wall impact is the likely clip moment. Repeat appeal is plausible if each new zone changes the decision I make. Recoloured bands and higher income alone would feel like the same chore farther from home. The long final-map return is a future boredom risk, even if the timer technically permits it.

Y hook/repeat: turning a corner during pursuit could feel exciting. It also makes a failed escape easier to blame on the camera or a hidden threat. Its v1 is only one strip, so the folded-map promise supplies little immediate reason to accept extra shipping work.

Z hook/repeat: the central home is easy to understand as a destination. Finding the next meaningful challenge around rings is less obvious. Crossing spokes could create funny encounters, but could also make a loss feel caused by somebody else's raid. That is a weak exchange for the added implementation burden.

Difficulty judgment: previews, visible commitment, recovery time, safe walking alternatives and protected banked progress are promising. None proves the difficulty will be enjoyable. The success percentages are tuning hypotheses, not player evidence. A vault's walking alternative must cost enough time to make the jump worth learning without making an accessibility choice feel like punishment.

Biggest objection: the promised first steal can become a queue. With one item per zone and eight players, an arriving player could watch somebody else take the only obvious objective before experiencing the hook. A three-second refill after resolution does not bound the wait during another player's unresolved carry. Competition before learning the escape can look like having nothing to do.

Minimal correction: keep X and the common systems, but make the first eligible steal reliably available for each newcomer during the first learning loop, with a clearly communicated allocation rule. Reuse the same first-zone pattern and keep rivals contesting later loose cargo. Do not add another map or a new combat system. This changes the shared-item rule and needs explicit design review; it is a correction proposal, not existing behavior.

Ship judgment: X is the strongest prototype candidate, not a demonstrated launch-ready game. Before multiplying content, observe whether a newcomer gets an available first objective, understands the tell, completes the steal-to-bank loop, and chooses another attempt. No pass rate is claimed here.

Lessons:
- When a clip sells an escape, make the first escape available immediately, because waiting cannot demonstrate the hook.
- When increasing difficulty, change a readable decision, because travel distance and colour changes alone offer little learning.
