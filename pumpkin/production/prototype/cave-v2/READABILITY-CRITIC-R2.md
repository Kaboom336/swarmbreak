# Independent visual readability critic — R2

Evidence: inspected only the five supplied final PNGs (portal, main-eye, cart-eye, cave-eye, far). No source, implementation history, documents or earlier reviews were read.

## Three-second portal result: PASS, with a weak middle choice

This is visual inference from a still image, **not a real new-player test**. A new player can plausibly identify three destinations within three seconds: green MAIN / CAMP on the left, yellow CART STATION in the middle, cyan CAVE on the right. The image does not establish which destination the game wants first; that requires task context or a player test.

| Choice | Route-mouth landmark | Traceability / obstruction |
|---|---|---|
| Main / camp | Large green sign above the left boardwalk | Strong: wide pale path rises left and remains visible. The sign post interrupts its center slightly but does not hide the entrance. |
| Cart station | Yellow sign and brown rails | Weakest: the yellow sign sits behind a crossing walkway, while the rails begin lower and farther right. A player must connect the label to the track rather than see one unmistakable mouth. Crates, posts and intersecting paths add clutter. |
| Cave | Cyan sign, bright cyan rectangular gateway, dark rock tunnel | Strong: gateway surrounds a visible pale entry path. The CAVE sign is offset toward the neighboring rail ramp, but the gateway and tunnel make the destination clear. |

The portal crop partly cuts off the arrival portal on the left, but it does not obstruct the three exits. Path intersections are visually busy; none of the three mouths is completely hidden. The main-eye view confirms a continuous, well-lit boardwalk. The cart-eye view confirms rails continuing up the ramp, though the huge foreground sign post blocks part of the destination and the station lacks an instantly recognizable cart/platform focal object. The cave-eye view is the cleanest destination entrance: broad path, luminous frame, tunnel lights visible inside.

## Look and coherence

- **Working:** warm pale walkways separate cleanly from cool blue-gray stone. Amber path lamps establish a consistent mine language. Brown rails provide a useful third material and direction cue. The tall magenta crystal gives the island a strong distant landmark.
- **Lighting:** traversal surfaces are bright and easy to read, but large pure-black gaps under bridges and inside the cave feel harsh against the evenly lit sky and stone. Bright yellow lamp cubes and cyan gateway strips look almost self-lit and compete with the crystals. The scene reads more like a stylized floating level than an enclosed cavern.
- **Crystal framing:** the far view has a distinctive cyan/magenta crystal fringe under the island and an effective tall summit crystal. At player height, several crystals dangle in empty sky under detached stepped rock caps; these feel like repeated decorations rather than mineral growth connected to a rock mass. They clutter the skyline without helping route choice.
- **Rock/mine coherence:** the far silhouette convincingly presents a floating rocky mine with a connected boardwalk network. At eye level, tiled stair-step hills resemble stacked masonry, while the cave is made of larger angular slabs. This scale/style difference is noticeable. The cyan doorway reads as a technological portal more than a timber-supported mine entrance; acceptable for a magical mine, less coherent for a natural cave.

## Highest-value corrections

1. Put the yellow station marker directly at the rail mouth, or add one visible cart/platform shape there. The label-to-route connection is the weakest of the three.
2. Reduce the visual bulk of nearby sign posts; the cart-eye post dominates the view and masks the scene beyond.
3. Add low ambient light to black voids and attach more skyline crystals visibly to stone. This would improve depth and material coherence without changing the route layout.

Lessons: When several paths cross, anchor each destination marker to its actual mouth because labels alone do not prove the route. When bright crystals define the theme, reduce competing emissive fixtures because every luminous element competes for attention. When rock styles change scale, transition them gradually because masonry-like hills and slab-like tunnels otherwise read as separate kits.
